3. Where in the day it goes wrong

Error is not uniform across the day. Both the model and the baseline are worst in the evening peak — which is where the merit order steepens, where the marginal plant is most likely to be a peaker, and where a battery makes most of its money.

4. The interval did not mean what it said

A P10–P90 band should contain the outturn 80% of the time. Fitted under pinball loss and evaluated out-of-sample, it does not come close.

Diagnosis first

The obvious conclusion is that the quantile model is broken. It is not. Measured on the data it was fitted on, coverage is 0.810 against a nominal 0.80 — exactly right.

Across that same split the standard deviation of prices rose from 14.8 to 21.3 GBP/MWh. The estimator is fine and the market moved. An interval calibrated on a calm window is simply too narrow for a volatile one.

Conformal prediction

Hold out a calibration window the model never saw, measure how far outside its own interval the outturn actually fell, and widen by the appropriate quantile of those misses. Under exchangeability this gives finite-sample coverage for any model.

Exchangeability does not hold on a non-stationary price series, so the calibration window is the most recent 60 days rather than a random sample, and coverage improves substantially without reaching nominal. That shortfall is the honest result.

IntervalCoverageNominal Mean widthWinklerCrossings

Read the Winkler score, not the coverage. Any interval reaches 100% coverage by becoming infinitely wide, and Winkler — width plus a penalty proportional to how far outside a miss landed — is the number that punishes exactly that. The conformalised band is wider and scores better, which is what says the extra width was earned rather than bought.

The Sharpe ratio here is about 10, and it is not a skill result. A battery buying low and selling high inside the same day is physical arbitrage — the naive schedule alone scores above 8. Quoting the Sharpe would be quoting the battery, not the forecast. The number that isolates the forecast's contribution is the — uplift over the naive schedule, and that is what this project claims.

Cumulative P&L

Net of all frictions. The curve steepens through the gas crisis because spreads widened, not because the model improved — which is the same reason the regime table above exists.

An earlier version of this strategy reported a Sharpe of 50

It traded the spread between the forecast and the seasonal naive against the spread between the realised price and the same naive. That number was not a discovery; it was a bug. No instrument pays that payoff — you cannot buy "the forecast error" — and the annualisation compounded it by treating 48 half-hourly settlement periods as 48 independent bets when they are one correlated day.

Both are fixed: the strategy is now a physical battery that could actually be operated, and annualisation uses √365.25 on daily P&L. The discarded design is written up in the docstring of strategy/signals.py and in lesson 11, because the reasoning is more useful than the strategy that replaced it.

6. What would break this in production

Weather is outturn, not forecast

Features come from Open-Meteo's archive — what the weather actually did, not what was forecast at gate closure. That makes the main run optimistic by an unknown amount. Re-running with weather removed entirely brackets it, and the honest range is between the two: closer to the with-weather end for temperature, closer to the without-weather end for wind.

Market impact is assumed away

One megawatt in a market that clears tens of gigawatts moves nothing, so treating the price as exogenous is defensible at this size and indefensible at a hundred times it. The backtest charges fixed slippage rather than modelling depth.

Perfect execution

Every scheduled MWh is assumed to trade at the settled price. A real operator faces the day-ahead auction clearing at a different price from the market index, and imbalance exposure on the difference.

No degradation cost

The battery cycles once a day for four and a half years with no cost charged for capacity fade. A real financing model prices cycles, and that materially changes whether a marginal day is worth trading.

7. Run it yourself

git clone https://github.com/nimb-ou/power-price-alpha
cd power-price-alpha
make venv
make all        # first run ~35 min: downloads 6.5 years, runs 54 folds

make fixtures   # or: a committed 6-month sample, offline, seconds
open reports/report.html

No API keys. Three public sources, all cached to parquet — the first run is slow and every run after it is offline. The committed CI fixture deliberately spans a clock-change day, because that is where the joins break.
