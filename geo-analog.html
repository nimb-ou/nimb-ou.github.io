1. Foundational Theory & Mathematics

Quantifying place identity transforms unstructured OpenStreetMap nodes into normalized 50-dimensional metric spaces.

Spatial Density Embedding

For each neighborhood bounded by radius \(R = 1.2\text{ km}\) centered at \((lat, lon)\), we extract POI counts across 50 categorized tags \(c_1, \dots, c_{50}\) (e.g., third-wave roaster, independent bookstore, craft brewery, art studio, transit hub). To prevent raw population size from confounding cultural distribution, counts are transformed with smoothed TF-IDF weighting:

$$v_i = \log(1 + \text{count}(c_i)) \cdot \log\left(\frac{1 + N_{\text{cities}}}{1 + \text{cities\_with}(c_i)}\right)$$

The normalized vector \(\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|_2\) enables scale-invariant neighborhood matching via cosine distance:

$$S(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|_2 \|\mathbf{v}\|_2} \in [-1, 1]$$

4. Failures, Trade-offs & Lessons Learned

The Absolute Density Bias

Early prototypes used raw POI counts. Consequently, Tokyo and Manhattan matched with everything because their absolute counts dwarfed mid-sized cities. Normalizing to unit vectors and applying IDF dampening restored cultural nuance.

Transit Disparities

European cities feature dense bus and subway stops that skewed American walkability comparisons. Weighting experiential amenities (cafes, bookshops, parks) higher than raw stop counts yielded significantly more intuitive matches.
